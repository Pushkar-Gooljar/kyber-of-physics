12The big picture

Every circular-motion question rests on one idea. An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, because its velocity keeps changing direction. By Newton's second law a resultant force must act. That force points to the centre, and it is supplied by something real: tension, friction, gravity, a contact force or a magnetic force.

Here are the six equations you must recall. The syllabus says "recall and use", so they are not on the formula sheet.

12.1Angles in radians

Definition · learn word for wordOne radian is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius of the circle.
Mark scheme wording

"angle (subtended at centre of a circle) when arc (length) = radius" B1 · s25/s24/w23 P42

This definition has appeared as a 1-mark (a) part in three of the worksheet papers. It's a free mark if you know it exactly.

Catching point
  • The question asks you to define the unit (the radian), not "angle in radians = s/r". The examiner report for s24/w23 makes this distinction.
  • Weaker candidates "defined" the radian as "180/π degrees". That is a conversion, not a definition, and gets 0.

Collins tip

Angular displacement is not linear displacement. After one complete lap the linear displacement is zero, but the angular displacement is 2π rad. The Earth's angular displacement around the Sun after one year is 2π rad.

Catching point · calculator mode

Check whether your calculator shows D or R before any sin, cos or tan. Lots of marks go here every series. Use degrees mode when the question gives angles in degrees (52°, 27°), and radians mode for x = R sin ωt.

Same angle, different distance

Every point on a rigid rotating body (a disc, a wheel, the Earth) sweeps out the same angular displacement in the same time. Points further from the axis travel further, so they have greater linear speed.

Examiner report

w25 P42 The common errors were taking the period as one year instead of one day, using the Earth's radius instead of the radius of the circle of latitude, and drawing the resultant force towards the centre of the Earth instead of the centre of the circle (horizontally, towards the axis).

12.1Angular speed

DefinitionAngular speed ω is the rate of change of angular displacement, i.e. the angle swept out by the radius per unit time.
ω = ΔθΔt
ω = 2πT = 2πf
v = rω

Units: rad s⁻¹. Angular speed is a scalar. Angular velocity is its vector version, but at A Level "angular speed" is the term the syllabus uses.

Deriving v = rω (Collins)

In one period T a point travels the circumference 2πr, so v = 2πr/T. Since ω = 2π/T, substitution gives v = rω. Equivalently, differentiate s = rθ with r constant.

Linked rotations: chains, belts and cogs

Two cogs joined by a chain are not on the same rigid body, so they don't share ω. Every link of the chain moves at the same linear speed, and the chain doesn't slip, so the rim of each cog moves at that speed too.

Examiner report · s25 P42
  • (b)(i): the common slip was dividing the bicycle's speed by the small cog's radius instead of the wheel's radius. The road speed is the speed of the wheel's rim.
  • (b)(iv): to turn the 0.24 m of chain into an angle on the large cog, use θ = s/r with the large cog's radius, giving 0.24/0.15 = 1.6 rad.
  • (c): for full marks, make a chain of reasoning. Same wheel ω → smaller cog radius → smaller chain speed → smaller pedal ω.

12.2Why a force towards the centre is needed

Velocity is a vector. In uniform circular motion the speed is constant but the direction changes continuously, so the velocity changes. A changing velocity is an acceleration, and by Newton's second law that needs a resultant force. By Newton's first law, without that force the object would carry on in a straight line along the tangent.

Mark scheme · "describe uniform circular motion in terms of velocity and acceleration"
  • velocity and acceleration both have constant magnitude (B1)
  • velocity is (always) perpendicular to acceleration (B1)

w25 P41 Q1(a). Examiner report: "It was rarely written that the magnitude of the acceleration was also constant."

A force always perpendicular to the motion

Collins builds the picture from a puck on ice. A push at right angles to the velocity changes its direction but not its speed. Repeat the push at equal intervals, each time perpendicular to the new velocity, and the path becomes a polygon. Make the pushes continuous and the path becomes a circle. A force of constant magnitude that is always perpendicular to the velocity therefore produces circular motion at constant speed and constant ω (syllabus 12.2.1–2).

Where does Δv point?

Interactive

Shrink Δθ. Δv = v₂ − v₁ swings round until it points straight at the centre, and the average acceleration |Δv|/Δt tends to exactly v²/r.

Show the derivation of a = v²/r (beyond the syllabus, but it explains the result)

The velocity vectors at the two ends of an arc have equal length v and are Δθ apart, so |Δv| = 2v sin(Δθ/2) ≈ vΔθ for small angles. The time taken is Δt = rΔθ/v. Therefore a = |Δv|/Δt = vΔθ · v/(rΔθ) = v²/r, directed along Δv, i.e. towards the centre.

Cut the string

Interactive

This is Collins question 10 (Figure 12.11). Once the string breaks the resultant force is zero. By Newton's first law the ball continues at constant velocity along the tangent. It doesn't fly radially outwards and it doesn't keep curving.

velocitytension = resultant force
Catching point · there is no centrifugal force

No outward force acts on an object moving in a circle. A passenger in a car going round a bend feels pushed outwards because of their own inertia: their body tends to carry on in a straight line. The car door pushes inwards on them to provide the centripetal force. By Newton's third law they push outwards on the door, but that force acts on the door, not on them. Writing "centrifugal force" in an answer is treated as wrong physics.

12.2Centripetal acceleration and force

DefinitionCentripetal force is the resultant force, directed towards the centre of the circle, needed to keep a body moving in uniform circular motion.
a = v²r = rω²
F = ma = mv²r = mrω²
  • Both F and a point towards the centre and are perpendicular to the velocity at every instant.
  • "Centripetal" is a description of direction, not the name of a new force. It is always the resultant of the real forces.
  • Because F ⊥ v, the centripetal force changes only the direction of motion. It does no work and the speed is unchanged.
Catching point · free-body diagrams

When asked to "draw the forces acting", never add an arrow labelled "centripetal force". m25 P42 Q1(a) The examiner report says "A large proportion of candidates thought that centripetal force was one of the two forces acting on the sphere". Draw only the real forces (weight, normal contact, tension, friction…) with their correct names.

Examiner report · w24 P42 Q1(a)

a = 0.85 × 140² = 16 660 m s⁻². Several candidates who had the right working wrote 1.6 × 10⁴ instead of 1.7 × 10⁴ m s⁻². Round properly, and don't truncate.

Worked example (SME): scaling

A ball on a string has r = 1.5 m and ω = 3.5 rad s⁻¹. If both r and ω are doubled, a = (2r)(2ω)² = 8rω², which is 8 × 1.5 × 3.5² = 147 m s⁻². For "what factor?" questions, write the proportionality first and then substitute.

12.2Resolving forces: cones, springs and conical pendulums

The highest-scoring Paper 4 questions put a mass on an angled support. The method is always the same:

  1. Draw the real forces only: weight, plus a tension or normal contact force at an angle.
  2. Vertically there is no acceleration, so the vertical component balances the weight.
  3. Horizontally, the horizontal component is the resultant force, so it equals mv²/r = mrω².
  4. Divide the two equations to eliminate the unknown force, giving tan θ = a/g.

extVertical circles

Vertical circles aren't named in the 12.2 syllabus statements, but they are a classic application that SME includes (the bucket of water). In a vertical circle the weight sometimes helps and sometimes opposes the tension. At the top and bottom both forces are vertical, so:

top: T + mg = mv²r
bottom: T − mg = mv²r

The tension is greatest at the bottom, which is where a string snaps. The minimum speed at the top occurs when T = 0, so the weight alone provides the centripetal force: mg = mv²/r gives vmin = √(gr). For SME's bucket on a 0.5 m string, √(9.81 × 0.5) = 2.21 m s⁻¹.

Ball on a string in a vertical circle

Interactive

m = 0.50 kg, r = 0.80 m, so √(gr) = 2.80 m s⁻¹. The speed changes round the loop because energy is conserved, so this motion is not uniform. Use T = mv²/r − mg cos φ, with φ measured from the top.

linkCircular motion seen side-on is SHM

w25 P41 Q1 joins this chapter to Chapter 17. Light a ball moving in a circle with parallel light and its shadow on a screen moves with simple harmonic motion. With θ = ωt, the shadow's displacement is x = R sin θ = R sin ωt.

PSExperimental skills: measuring the centripetal force

Method points examiners reward

  • Keep r constant: fix a paper-clip marker just below the tube and whirl so the marker stays just clear of the tube's bottom end.
  • Measure T: time 10 (or 20) revolutions with a stopwatch and divide. Repeat and average. Then v = 2πr/T.
  • F: the tension equals Mg, assuming no friction at the tube and that the string is horizontal.
  • Safety: wear eye protection, keep clear of other people, check the knot, and use a soft rubber ball.
  • Analysis: plot F against v². This should be a straight line through the origin with gradient m/r.

Analysing the Collins data (Table 12.2, r = 0.30 m, m = 45 g)

Graph

The theory predicts a gradient of m/r = 0.045/0.30 = 0.150 kg m⁻¹. The data give about 0.105, roughly 30 % low.

Why is the gradient low? A low gradient means the measured F (= Mg) is smaller than the real centripetal force for a given v. Two likely causes: friction at the tube's edge, so the string tension on the ball's side exceeds Mg; and r drifting larger than 0.30 m. A string sloping below the horizontal would also make the ball's real radius less than the measured length. Large random errors come from timing by hand, so time more revolutions.

Top catching points

Radius traps
  • Use the radius of the actual circle. For latitude that's R cos λ. For clay 1.2 cm in from the rim of a 9.3 cm disc it's 8.1 cm (s24). For star A it's d/3.
  • A diameter is given surprisingly often: the 18 cm bowl in Collins and the 0.46 m circle in w25 P41.
  • Don't use the arc length as the radius. That happened in w23 (0.44 m is a distance travelled; L = 0.18 m).
Time traps
  • The Earth spins once per day (86 400 s), not once a year.
  • A minute hand takes 1 hour, not 1 minute (w23 ER). A second hand takes 60 s.
  • Convert ms → s and rpm → rad s⁻¹ before substituting.
Diagram traps
  • A velocity arrow must be a straight tangent, not curved. A curved arrow "implies multiple directions" (s24 ER).
  • The acceleration arrow points along the radius to the centre.
  • Never draw a "centripetal force" as an extra force, and never draw a centrifugal force.
  • Label every arrow if the question says "labelled" (V, A, "weight").
Exam-craft traps
  • "Show that" answers need a full substitution and a final value to more s.f. than given (3.90 × 10⁶ from 6.37 × 10⁶ × cos 52.2°).
  • "Use your answer to (b)(ii)": do exactly that (w25 P44).
  • "In terms of v and ω": no other symbols should appear.
  • Do a quick plausibility check. Error carried forward still produced implausible values of L in w23.

QQuick-fire practice (Collins Ch 12 questions)

Answers are worked out from the Collins questions. Tap a question to check yours.

Children on a roundabout: angular displacement for one rotation, at the edge and near the axis? Distance at r = 1.3 m and 0.80 m?
Both have angular displacement 2π rad. The distances are 2π × 1.3 = 8.2 m and 2π × 0.80 = 5.0 m.
Angular displacement of a point on the equator in 1 hour?
2π/24 = π/12 rad = 0.262 rad
Minute hand (5.5 cm) over 3.5 hours: angle and tip distance?
θ = 3.5 × 2π = 7π = 22.0 rad; s = rθ = 0.055 × 22.0 = 1.21 m
Second hand: ω? Linear speed 2.0 cm from the axis?
ω = 2π/60 = 0.105 rad s⁻¹; v = 0.020 × 0.105 = 2.1 × 10⁻³ m s⁻¹
Earth's spin: ω, speed at the equator (R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m), speed at 80° N (r = 1.1 × 10⁶ m)?
ω = 2π/86 400 = 7.3 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹; equator 465 m s⁻¹; 80° N 80 m s⁻¹
Mercury: v = 4.7 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹, r = 5.8 × 10¹⁰ m. ω and orbital period?
ω = v/r = 8.1 × 10⁻⁷ rad s⁻¹; T = 2π/ω = 7.8 × 10⁶ s ≈ 90 days
★ Electron "spinning" at 3 × 10²⁹ rad s⁻¹ with diameter 10⁻¹⁸ m: why is this impossible?
v = rω = 0.5 × 10⁻¹⁸ × 3 × 10²⁹ = 1.5 × 10¹¹ m s⁻¹, which is 500 times the speed of light. Electrons can't literally be spinning spheres.
Motorcycle engine at 16 000 rpm: ω? Speed of a point 12 cm from the crankshaft axis?
ω = 16 000 × 2π/60 = 1.68 × 10³ rad s⁻¹; v = 0.12 × 1676 = 201 m s⁻¹
Ball rolls at 2.3 m s⁻¹ round a bowl in a path of diameter 18 cm: revolutions per second and ω?
Circumference π × 0.18 = 0.565 m, so 2.3/0.565 = 4.07 → 4 complete revs; ω = 2.3/0.09 = 25.6 rad s⁻¹. Use the radius, not the diameter.
Name the force that keeps (i) the Earth round the Sun, (ii) clothes in a spinning drum, (iii) a ball whirled on a string.
(i) the Sun's gravitational pull; (ii) the normal contact force from the drum wall; (iii) the tension in the string. Each acts towards the centre of the circle.
A bird flies at ω = 5.25 rad s⁻¹ in a circle of radius 650 m (SME). Linear speed and frequency?
v = 650 × 5.25 = 3410 m s⁻¹ (an unrealistic bird); f = ω/2π = 0.836 Hz
