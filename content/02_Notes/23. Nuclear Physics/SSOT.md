## 23.1 Mass Defect and Nuclear Binding Energy

> [!concept] Equivalence of Mass and Energy
> In 1905, Albert Einstein published his research showing that matter can be considered a form of energy. He proposed that mass and energy are entirely interchangeable. 
> *   Mass can be converted into energy (e.g., nuclear fusion in the sun, nuclear power plants, particle accelerators).
> *   Energy can be converted into mass.
> 
> According to Einstein, since the decay of any radioactive nucleus releases energy, the total mass before the decay MUST exceed the total mass after the decay. The difference in mass before and after the decay is entirely equivalent to the total kinetic and electromagnetic energy of the decay products.

> [!math] Mass-Energy Equivalence Equation
> $$E = mc^2$$
> Where:
> *   $E$ = Energy (J)
> *   $m$ = Mass (kg)
> *   $c$ = Speed of light ($3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$)
> 
> *Atomic Mass Unit Conversion:*
> Atomic, nuclear, and particle masses are usually expressed in unified atomic mass units (u). 
> $$1 \text{ u} = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}$$
> The energy equivalent of $1 \text{ u}$ is exactly **$931 \text{ MeV}$**. This enables a direct conversion from mass in u to energy in MeV without needing to use $E = mc^2$ in kg and Joules first.

> [!concept] Representing Simple Nuclear Reactions
> A nucleus can be described using standard notation $\text{}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$. 
> *   **Nucleon Number (A):** The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus (top number).
> *   **Proton Number (Z):** The total number of protons in the nucleus (bottom number).
> 
> When writing nuclear equations, the total nucleon number (A) and total proton number (Z) must be conserved on both sides of the equation.
> 
> ![AZX Notation representing the nucleon and proton number of a chemical symbol|500](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/10/11.1.3-AZX-Notation.png)

> [!info] Definition: Mass Defect
> The difference between the mass of a nucleus and the total mass of its constituent nucleons when they are infinitely separated.
> *Source: 9702_w25_qp_42 - Mark Scheme*

> [!warning] Common Pitfall
> The definition of mass defect is frequently misunderstood. Do not define it as a "change in mass during a nuclear reaction", nor describe it as an "energy". It is strictly the difference in mass between the intact nucleus and its infinitely separated nucleons. (*Source: Examiner Report 9702_w25_qp_42*)

> [!math] Calculating Mass Defect
> $$\Delta m = Zm_p + (A - Z)m_n - m_{total}$$
> Where:
> *   $Z$ = proton number
> *   $A$ = nucleon number
> *   $m_p$ = mass of a proton
> *   $m_n$ = mass of a neutron
> *   $m_{total}$ = measured mass of the intact nucleus

> [!concept] Nuclear Binding Energy
> A system of separated nucleons has a greater mass than a system of bound nucleons. Because nuclei are made up of neutrons and protons, there are forces of electrostatic repulsion between the positive protons. Therefore, it takes energy to hold nucleons together as a nucleus.
> Energy and mass are proportional, so the total energy of a nucleus is less than the sum of the energies of its constituent nucleons. The formation of a nucleus from isolated nucleons is an exothermic reaction (it releases energy).

> [!info] Definition: Nuclear Binding Energy
> The (minimum) energy required to separate all the nucleons in a nucleus to infinity.
> *Source: 9702_s24_qp_42 - Mark Scheme*

> [!danger] Critical Information
> Avoid describing binding energy as the "energy stored in the nucleus". This is incorrect physics. It is the energy that must be **put into** the nucleus to separate all the nucleons against the strong nuclear force.

> [!concept] Binding Energy per Nucleon
> In order to compare nuclear stability between different elements, it is more useful to look at the **binding energy per nucleon**. 
> *   Binding energy per nucleon = Total Binding Energy / Nucleon Number (A).
> *   A higher binding energy per nucleon indicates a higher stability (more energy is required to separate the nucleons).
> 
> ![Graph of binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number showing the peak at Iron-56|500](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2021/05/23.1-Binding-Energy-per-Nucleon.png)
> 
> **Key features of the graph:**
> 1.  **Low values of A:** Light nuclei have lower binding energies per nucleon. They have weaker electrostatic forces and will undergo **fusion** to become more stable. The gradient is very steep. Helium-4 ($^4_2\text{He}$), Carbon-12 ($^{12}_6\text{C}$), and Oxygen-16 ($^{16}_8\text{O}$) are anomalies; they do not fit the smooth trend and have exceptionally high BE/nucleon for their size.
> 2.  **Peak at A $\approx$ 56:** Iron-56 has the highest binding energy per nucleon (approx 8.8 MeV), making it the most stable of all elements.
> 3.  **High values of A:** The heaviest elements are unstable and will undergo **fission**. The gradient is much less steep here. Fission reactions release less binding energy per nucleon than fusion reactions.

> [!tip] Examiner Advice for Graph Sketching
> If asked to sketch the Binding Energy per Nucleon graph:
> *   Start the curve at A = 1 (do not start at A = 0, as $A=0$ is not a nucleus!).
> *   Draw a steep initial positive gradient on the left.
> *   Create a rounded peak around A = 56 (Iron).
> *   Draw a shallower, continually decreasing negative gradient for higher A values.
> *   The line must **not** drop back down to 0 binding energy.

> [!info] Definition: Nuclear Fusion & Fission
> **Nuclear Fusion:** Two (small) nuclei join together to form one larger nucleus. (*Source: 9702_w23_qp_41*)
> **Nuclear Fission:** A single large nucleus splits/divides to form two (or more) smaller nuclei. (*Source: Synthesized from SME/Collins & MS 9702_w24_qp_42*)

> [!concept] Nuclear Fission and Fusion Explained
> **Nuclear Fusion:**
> Fusion requires extremely high temperatures (like the core of a star) because both nuclei are positively charged. They electrostatically repel each other strongly. High kinetic energy is needed to force the nuclei close enough together (to within $10^{-15}\text{ m}$) so that the short-range attractive strong nuclear force can overcome the electrostatic repulsion and bind them.
> 
> ![Diagram showing deuterium and tritium fusing to form helium, releasing a neutron and energy|500](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2021/05/23.1-Nuclear-Fusion.png)
> 
> **Nuclear Fission:**
> Usually initiated by neutron bombardment. An unstable heavy nucleus absorbs a slow-moving neutron, becomes highly unstable, and splits into fission fragments (daughter nuclei), releasing energy and more neutrons (which can trigger a cascade/chain reaction).
> 
> ![Diagram showing a neutron striking a target nucleus causing it to split into two fission products and three neutrons|500](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2021/05/23.1-Nuclear-fission.png)
> 
> **Why both release energy:**
> In both processes, the products formed have a *higher binding energy per nucleon* than the reactants. This means the products are more tightly bound and stable. Consequently, the total mass of the products is strictly less than the total mass of the reactants. This mass defect is converted entirely into energy ($E = mc^2$), appearing primarily as the kinetic energy of the products/fragments and gamma radiation.

> [!concept] Calculating Energy Released
> The energy released in a nuclear reaction can be calculated using the mass-energy equivalence formula: 
> $$E = c^2 \Delta m$$
> 
> **Steps for calculation:**
> 1. Calculate the total mass of reactants.
> 2. Calculate the total mass of products.
> 3. Find the mass difference ($\Delta m = \text{mass}_{reactants} - \text{mass}_{products}$).
> 4. Convert $\Delta m$ from unified atomic mass units (u) into kg (multiply by $1.66 \times 10^{-27}$).
> 5. Multiply by $c^2$ to find Energy in Joules. 
> *(Alternatively, multiply mass difference in 'u' directly by 931 to get energy in MeV).*

## 23.2 Radioactive Decay

> [!abstract] Syllabus 23.2 Radioactive decay
> **Candidates should be able to:**
> 1. understand that fluctuations in count rate provide evidence for the random nature of radioactive decay
> 2. understand that radioactive decay is both spontaneous and random
> 3. define activity and decay constant, and recall and use $A = \lambda N$
> 4. define half-life
> 5. use $\lambda = 0.693 / t_{1/2}$
> 6. understand the exponential nature of radioactive decay, and sketch and use the relationship $x = x_0e^{-\lambda t}$, where $x$ could represent activity, number of undecayed nuclei or received count rate

> [!concept] Random and Spontaneous Nature of Decay
> Radioactive decay is the spontaneous disintegration of a nucleus to form a more stable nucleus, resulting in the emission of an alpha, beta, or gamma particle.
> 
> **Random Nature:**
> Radioactive decay is random. A random process is one in which the exact time of decay of a specific nucleus cannot be predicted. Instead, every undecayed nucleus has a constant probability of decaying in a given time interval. 
> *Evidence:* When a Geiger-Muller (GM) tube is placed near a radioactive source, the count rate fluctuates irregularly.
> 
> **Spontaneous Nature:**
> Radioactive decay is spontaneous. A spontaneous process is one which cannot be influenced by environmental factors such as temperature, pressure, or chemical conditions.

> [!info] Definitions: Random and Spontaneous
> **Random:** Cannot predict when a particular nucleus will decay OR cannot predict which nucleus will decay next. 
> *Source: 9702_w24_qp_42 - Mark Scheme*
> **Spontaneous:** (Decay is) not affected by external / environmental factors.
> *Source: 9702_s25_qp_42 - Mark Scheme*

> [!tip] Examiner Advice
> When defining "random", weaker candidates mistakenly think *everything* about radioactive decay is unpredictable. This is false. Unpredictability is only at the level of the *individual* nuclei. On a macroscopic scale with a large number of nuclei, the decay rate is highly mathematically predictable! (*Source: ER 9702_w24_qp_42*)

> [!info] Definition: Activity
> The number of nuclear disintegrations per unit time. 
> *Source: 9702_s25_qp_41 - Mark Scheme*
> *Unit:* Becquerel (Bq). $1 \text{ Bq} = 1 \text{ decay per second}$.

> [!info] Definition: Decay Constant ($\lambda$)
> The probability of decay of a nucleus per unit time.
> *Source: Synthesized from SME/Collins*

> [!math] Activity Equation
> $$A = \lambda N$$
> Where:
> *   $A$ = Activity (Bq)
> *   $\lambda$ = Decay constant (s$^{-1}$)
> *   $N$ = Number of undecayed nuclei in the sample

> [!info] Definition: Half-life
> The time for the activity (of a sample) to halve.
> *Source: 9702_s24_qp_41 - Mark Scheme*

> [!warning] Common Pitfall
> Do not define half-life as "the time for half the nuclei to decay." If an isotope decays into another radioactive daughter isotope, the total number of radioactive nuclei might not halve. The safest and most rigorous definition accepted by examiners is "the time taken for the **activity** to halve."

> [!math] Half-life Equation
> $$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$$
> Where:
> *   $t_{1/2}$ = Half-life
> *   $\lambda$ = Decay constant

> [!concept] Exponential Nature of Radioactive Decay
> In radioactive decay, the number of undecayed nuclei $N$, the activity $A$, and the count rate $C$ all decrease exponentially with time. This means they fall by a constant ratio in equal time intervals.
> 
> ![Exponential Decay Graph showing shallow and steep decay curves based on decay constant size|500](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2021/05/23.2-Exponential-Decay-Graph.png)

> [!math] Decay Equations
> $$N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$
> $$A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$
> $$C = C_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$
> Taking the natural log ($\ln$) of both sides yields a linear equation (e.g., $\ln A = \ln A_0 - \lambda t$). A graph of $\ln A$ vs $t$ produces a straight line where the **magnitude of the gradient** is the decay constant $\lambda$.

> **Question:**
> A pure sample of polonium-193 contains $N_0$ nuclei. After time $t$ it contains $N$ nuclei. The variation of $\ln(N/N_0)$ with $t$ is a straight line graph with a negative gradient.
> (i) State the name of the quantity that is represented by the magnitude of the gradient of the line.
> (ii) The line passes through (0, 0) and (0.84 ms, -1.4). Use the graph to determine the half-life, in ms.
> 
> **Mark Scheme:**
> (i) decay constant (1)
> (ii) 
> *   decay constant = magnitude of gradient = $1.4 / 0.84$ (C1)
> *   $\text{half-life} = \ln 2 / (1.4 / 0.84) = 0.42 \text{ ms}$ (A1)

> [!question] 9702_w22_qp_41_qu_010 (b)
> **Question:**
> Fig. 10.1 shows the variation with $t$ of the value of $\ln(M/10^{-16} \text{g})$ where $M$ is the mass of Carbon-15. The plotted points scatter slightly around a linear trend.
> (i) State how Fig. 10.1 demonstrates that radioactive decay is random.
> (iii) Show that the decay constant $\lambda$ is given by the magnitude of the gradient of your line of best fit.
> 
> **Mark Scheme:**
> (i) line fluctuates OR trend is a straight line (1)
> (iii) 
> *   $M = M_0 e^{-\lambda t}$
> *   so $\ln M = \ln M_0 - \lambda t$ so gradient = $-\lambda$ (and magnitude of gradient = $\lambda$) (1)

> [!question] 9702_s25_qp_42_qu_010 (b)
> **Question:**
> Two radioactive isotopes X and Y decay to stable isotopes. X has initial activity $4A$ and half-life $2T$. Y has initial activity $A$ and half-life $3T$.
> Determine, in terms of $T$, the time at which the two samples will have equal activities.
> 
> **Mark Scheme:**
> *   correct substitution of $A_0$ and $\lambda$ into $A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ for sample X or Y (C1)
> *   $4A e^{-t \ln 2 / 2T} = A e^{-t \ln 2 / 3T}$ (C1)
> *   $t = 12T$ (A1)

> [!question] 9702_m23_qp_42_qu_008 (b)
> **Question:**
> The power source in a space probe contains 0.874 kg of Plutonium-238. Each nucleus that decays emits 5.59 MeV. The half-life is 87.7 years. 
> (i) Calculate initial number $N_0$ of Pu-238 nuclei.
> (ii) Determine initial activity.
> (iii) Determine initial power output.
> (iv) Calculate the time, in years, until the power output decreases to 65.3% of its initial value.
> 
> **Mark Scheme:**
> (i) $N_0 = 0.874 / (238 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27}) = 2.21 \times 10^{24}$ (A1)
> (ii) $A = \lambda N \rightarrow [(\ln 2)/(87.7 \times 365 \times 24 \times 3600)] \times 2.21 \times 10^{24} = 5.54 \times 10^{14} \text{ Bq}$ (C1, A1)
> (iii) $\text{Power} = 5.54 \times 10^{14} \times 5.59 \times 10^6 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 496 \text{ W}$ (C1, A1)
> (iv) 
> *   $65.3 = 100 e^{-(\ln 2 / 87.7)t}$ (C1)
> *   $\ln(0.653) = -(\ln 2 / 87.7)t \rightarrow t = 53.9 \text{ years}$ (A1)
> 
> **Examiner Report:**
> (iv) Premature rounding of intermediate values leads to incorrect answers in the final significant figure, and this was a common problem in this question. The data justifies a 3 significant figure answer. Candidates who used a value of decay constant prematurely rounded to 3sf often arrived at an answer that was not quite correct.
