# Chapter 7: Waves

## 7.1 Progressive Waves

### Wave Motion & Energy Transfer
A **progressive wave** transfers **energy** from one place to another without transferring matter. The particles in the medium simply oscillate back and forth about fixed equilibrium positions.
*   **Mechanical Waves:** Require a medium to travel through (e.g., sound waves, water waves, waves on a string). 
*   **Electromagnetic Waves:** Can travel through a vacuum (e.g., light, X-rays).

Wave motion can be demonstrated visually in laboratories:
*   **Vibration in Ropes:** Flickening a rope up and down creates a transverse wave.
*   **Vibration in Springs (Slinky):** Pushing and pulling a spring horizontally creates a longitudinal wave.
*   **Ripple Tanks:** A vibrating paddle creates transverse water waves. These can be used to study reflection, refraction, and diffraction using shadows projected onto a screen beneath the tank.

![Wave on a rope and spring](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/09/7.1.1.1-Wave-on-rope-and-spring.png)

### Key Wave Properties
> **Definition Checklist**
> *   **Displacement ($x$):** The distance of a point on the wave from its equilibrium position. It is a vector quantity (can be positive or negative).
> *   **Amplitude ($A$):** The *maximum* displacement of a particle in the wave from its equilibrium position.
> *   **Period ($T$):** The time taken for one complete oscillation or wave cycle (measured in seconds, $\text{s}$).
> *   **Frequency ($f$):** The number of complete oscillations passing a point per unit time (measured in Hertz, $\text{Hz}$, or $\text{s}^{-1}$). $f = \frac{1}{T}$
> *   **Wavelength ($\lambda$):** The minimum distance between two points on the wave that are oscillating exactly in phase (e.g., peak to peak).
> *   **Wave Speed ($v$):** The distance travelled by the wave energy per unit time ($\text{m s}^{-1}$).

![Transverse Wave Properties](https://s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/elements.cognitoedu.org/754b7ff8-e15c-4d5a-8dac-6a2ab989633b/transverse-wave-diagram.png)

### Phase Difference
**Phase difference** tells us how much a point or a wave is in front of, or behind, another. It is measured in degrees, radians, or fractions of a wavelength ($\lambda$).
*   **In phase:** Particles are at the same point in their cycle (e.g., two crests). Phase difference = $0^\circ, 360^\circ$, or $2\pi \text{ rad}$.
*   **Anti-phase:** Particles are moving in exactly opposite directions. Phase difference = $180^\circ$, or $\pi \text{ rad}$ (equivalent to $\frac{1}{2}\lambda$).

![Phase Difference](https://s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/elements.cognitoedu.org/4a946819-2906-49c7-a2a6-89b184966026/wave-phase-difference.png)

### Deriving the Wave Equation
By using the standard equation for speed:
$$Speed = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}$$
If we consider one complete wave cycle, the distance travelled is the **wavelength ($\lambda$)** and the time taken is the **period ($T$)**.
$$v = \frac{\lambda}{T}$$
Since $f = \frac{1}{T}$, we can substitute frequency into the equation to get the **Wave Equation**:
$$v = f \lambda$$

### Using a Cathode-Ray Oscilloscope (CRO)
A CRO displays sound waves and alternating currents as a transverse trace on a screen. 
*   **Time-base (x-axis):** Determines the time represented by each horizontal division (e.g., $\text{ms div}^{-1}$ or $\mu\text{s cm}^{-1}$). It is used to find the **Period ($T$)** and thereby the frequency.
*   **Y-gain (y-axis):** Determines the voltage represented by each vertical division (e.g., $\text{V div}^{-1}$). It is used to find the **Amplitude**.

> **💡 Examiner Tip - Finding Frequency from a CRO:**
> 1. Count the number of horizontal divisions for **one full cycle** (peak to peak).
> 2. Multiply by the time-base setting to find the Period ($T$). *Be very careful with unit prefixes (e.g., convert ms to s).*
> 3. Use $f = \frac{1}{T}$ to find the frequency.

![CRO Diagram](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/09/7.1.1.3-CRO-diagram.png)

### Wave Intensity
Intensity is the rate of energy transfer per unit area. It is defined as:
$$Intensity = \frac{Power}{Area}$$
$$I = \frac{P}{A}$$
*   Measured in Watts per square metre ($\text{W m}^{-2}$).
*   **Inverse Square Law:** For spherical waves spreading from a point source, the area is $4\pi r^2$. Therefore, **$I \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$**. If you double the distance from the source, the intensity drops to $1/4$.

**Intensity & Amplitude:**
The intensity of a progressive wave is directly proportional to the **square of its amplitude** (and the square of its frequency).
$$I \propto A^2$$
*(If the amplitude is halved, the intensity decreases by a factor of 4.)*

## 7.2 Transverse and Longitudinal Waves

### Comparing Wave Types
| Feature | Transverse Waves | Longitudinal Waves |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Oscillations** | Perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the direction of energy transfer | Parallel to the direction of energy transfer |
| **Structure** | Made of **Peaks** and **Troughs** | Made of **Compressions** (high pressure/density) and **Rarefactions** (low pressure/density) |
| **Vacuum** | Electromagnetic waves *can* travel through a vacuum | *Cannot* travel through a vacuum (require a medium) |
| **Polarisation** | **Can** be polarised | **Cannot** be polarised |
| **Examples** | Light, ripples on water, S-waves | Sound, ultrasound, P-waves |

![Transverse Wave](https://s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/elements.cognitoedu.org/bba16eee-abc1-4e08-a0ce-236cbfde1c9b/transverse-wave-diagram.png)
![Longitudinal Wave](https://s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/elements.cognitoedu.org/06dd4631-a792-4313-b1f3-ab67a31e3315/longitudinal-waves-diagram.png)

### Graphical Representations
Both types of waves can be represented on graphs. 
*   **Displacement-Distance Graph:** A "snapshot" of the wave in space. The distance between two successive peaks is the **wavelength ($\lambda$)**.
*   **Displacement-Time Graph:** The motion of a *single particle* over time. The time between two successive peaks is the **period ($T$)**. 

> **💡 Examiner Tip:** Always check the **x-axis** of a wave graph! If it is distance, you can find the wavelength. If it is time, you can find the period. Never confuse the two!

## 7.3 Doppler Effect for Sound Waves

The Doppler effect is the apparent change in observed frequency and wavelength when a source of waves moves relative to a stationary observer.
*   **Moving Towards:** Wavelengths bunch up (compress) $\rightarrow$ Wavelength decreases, **Frequency increases** (higher pitch).
*   **Moving Away:** Wavelengths stretch out $\rightarrow$ Wavelength increases, **Frequency decreases** (lower pitch).

![Doppler Effect Stationary](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/09/7.1.3.1-Doppler-shift-diagram-1.png)
![Doppler Effect Moving](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2023/10/8-2-2-4-double-slit-equation.png) *(Note: Waves compress in front of the moving source and spread out behind it)*

### The Doppler Shift Equation
For a moving source and a **stationary observer**, the observed frequency ($f_o$) is calculated using:
$$f_o = f_s \left( \frac{v}{v \pm v_s} \right)$$
Where:
*   $f_o$ = observed frequency ($\text{Hz}$)
*   $f_s$ = frequency of the source ($\text{Hz}$)
*   $v$ = speed of the wave in the medium (e.g., speed of sound $\approx 340 \text{ m s}^{-1}$)
*   $v_s$ = speed of the source relative to the observer ($\text{m s}^{-1}$)

**Sign Convention ($\pm$):**
*   Use **minus ($-$)** if the source is moving **towards** the observer (denominator gets smaller, making $f_o$ larger).
*   Use **plus ($+$)** if the source is moving **away** from the observer (denominator gets larger, making $f_o$ smaller).

## 7.4 Electromagnetic Spectrum

All electromagnetic (EM) waves share the following properties:
1. They are all **transverse waves** consisting of oscillating electric and magnetic fields.
2. They travel at the **same speed in a vacuum** (free space), known as $c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$.
3. They can travel through a vacuum (do not require a medium).

![EM Spectrum](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2022/05/4-2-3-electromagnetic-spectrum_sl-physics-rn.png)

### Wavelength Ranges
You must memorize the approximate order and wavelength bounds of the EM spectrum in free space:

| Region | Approximate Wavelength Range ($\text{m}$) |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Radio Waves** | $> 0.1$ |
| **Microwaves** | $0.1 \text{ to } 10^{-3}$ |
| **Infrared (IR)** | $10^{-3} \text{ to } 7 \times 10^{-7}$ |
| **Visible Light** | $7 \times 10^{-7} \text{ to } 4 \times 10^{-7}$ **(700 nm to 400 nm)** |
| **Ultraviolet (UV)** | $4 \times 10^{-7} \text{ to } 10^{-8}$ |
| **X-rays** | $10^{-8} \text{ to } 10^{-13}$ |
| **Gamma ($\gamma$) rays**| $10^{-10} \text{ to } 10^{-16}$ *(Overlaps with X-rays)* |

> **💡 Examiner Tip:** You only need to memorize the *wavelengths*. If an exam question asks for a frequency range, quickly calculate it using $c = f\lambda$ where $c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$.

## 7.5 Polarisation

Polarisation is a phenomenon that **only occurs in transverse waves**. It is proof that a wave is transverse (e.g., light can be polarised, sound cannot).
*   **Unpolarised wave:** Oscillates in *all* planes perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
*   **Polarised wave:** Oscillations are restricted to a **single plane**.

![Unpolarised vs Polarised](https://s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/elements.cognitoedu.org/877eb87a-32a6-4151-9075-77b636801a0d/polarisation-of-waves-diagram.png)

### Polarisers and Analysers
A **polarising filter** acts like a slit, only allowing wave oscillations parallel to its "transmission axis" to pass through. 
*   When unpolarised light passes through an initial polariser, it becomes plane-polarised, and its intensity strictly halves: $I = \frac{1}{2} I_0$.
*   A second polarising filter placed in the path of already polarised light is called an **analyser**. 
*   If the analyser is rotated $90^\circ$ relative to the first polariser (crossed polarisers), **no light is transmitted** ($I = 0$).

![Action of Polarising Filters](https://s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/elements.cognitoedu.org/1aabdf48-2b2f-4b07-b6ae-1ac2c3982637/polarising-waves-diagram.png)

### Malus's Law
To calculate the intensity of a plane-polarised electromagnetic wave after it transmits through an analyser, we use Malus's Law:
$$I = I_0 \cos^2\theta$$
Where:
*   $I$ = Transmitted intensity
*   $I_0$ = Initial intensity of the *already polarised* incident wave
*   $\theta$ = Angle between the transmission axis of the polariser and the plane of polarisation of the incident wave.

![Malus Law Graph](https://s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/elements.cognitoedu.org/9b79dcda-fd6f-46f4-b2d4-348ebf769b78/light-intensity-polarisation-angle-graph.png)

> **💡 Examiner Tip on Malus's Law:** 
> Read the question carefully! Does it start with *unpolarised* light or *polarised* light? 
> If the light starts **unpolarised**, remember to halve its intensity as it goes through the *first* filter. You only apply $I = I_0 \cos^2\theta$ for the *second* filter (the analyser).
