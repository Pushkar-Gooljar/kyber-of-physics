# [[Causes and Drivers of 21st-Century Warfare]]

> [!summary] Core Analytical Thesis
> The 21st century has seen a structural shift away from the post-Cold War unipolar peace. Modern warfare is no longer solely driven by traditional conquest, but by a complex matrix of **multipolar geopolitical competition, severe resource scarcity, the collapse of global governance, and territorial irredentism**. These drivers have pushed global military spending to an unprecedented **$2.89 trillion (2.5% of global GDP) in 2025**.

---

## 🌍 1. Multipolarity, Hegemony, and Proxy Warfare

As the "unipolar moment" of undisputed US dominance fades, rising and revisionist powers are actively contesting the global security architecture to establish regional hegemony.

> [!info] The Return of Great Power Competition
> Direct kinetic conflict between nuclear-armed superpowers carries the risk of mutually assured destruction (MAD). Therefore, superpowers aggressively utilize **Proxy Warfare**—funding, arming, and directing third-party states or non-state actors to bleed their rivals without engaging in direct combat.

> [!abstract] Case Study: The Russia-Ukraine Conflict (2022–Present)
> Ukraine serves as the primary site of industrial-scale proxy competition between Russia and the Western alliance. 
> - **Economic Asymmetry:** To prevent Russian hegemony in Eastern Europe, the West injected extraordinary military aid (€72.3B from the EU, €64.3B from the US). This allowed Ukraine to sustain a military burden of **$84.1 billion in 2025 (40% of its GDP)**.
> - **The Driver:** Russia's desire to secure a geographical buffer zone and re-establish a sphere of influence, clashing directly with NATO's eastward expansion and Ukraine's sovereign alignment.

## 💧 2. Resource Scarcity and Eco-Warfare (Hydropolitics)

Climate change, exponential population growth, and hyper-industrialization have transformed essential natural resources into zero-sum strategic assets.

> [!info] Transboundary Water Disputes (Hydro-Hegemony)
> As global freshwater supplies shrink, upstream nations weaponize geography by building mega-dams, effectively holding downstream nations hostage. Water is increasingly viewed as a primary trigger for state-on-state conflict.

> [!danger] Case Study: The Blue Nile & GERD
> - **The Driver:** Ethiopia's construction of the **Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD)** (74 Billion Cubic Meters capacity) to alleviate domestic energy poverty. 
> - **The Threat:** The Blue Nile supplies **86% of the water** reaching downstream Egypt (a country that relies on the Nile for 97% of its freshwater). Hydrological models project up to a **25% reduction in downstream flow** during filling/drought phases. The lack of a binding treaty creates an existential flashpoint for conflict in North Africa.

> [!info] Rare Earth Minerals and Energy Chokepoints
> The transition to green energy and advanced military tech (EVs, lasers, AI chips, drones) requires critical minerals (lithium, cobalt, semiconductors). Securing supply chains in Africa, South America, and the Indo-Pacific is driving intense neo-colonial competition and internal destabilization.

## 🗺️ 3. Territorial Irredentism and Grey-Zone Expansion

Irredentism—the desire to reclaim "lost" or historical territory—remains a potent nationalist driver for war, modernized through sub-kinetic tactics.

> [!info] Grey-Zone Operations
> Coercive state actions that remain deliberately below the threshold of open, kinetic military conflict. States use incremental aggression ("salami-slicing") to alter borders without triggering international defense treaties.

> [!danger] Case Study: The South China Sea
> - **The Driver:** China's pursuit of absolute regional dominance and control over critical global shipping lanes and undersea hydrocarbon reserves.
> - **The Tactic:** Beijing deploys the **People's Armed Forces Maritime Militia (PAFMM)**—subsidized commercial fishing vessels integrated into military command structures. They blockade access to the Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZs) of the Philippines, Vietnam, and Malaysia, asserting *de facto* sovereignty without firing a shot or triggering US treaty interventions.

> [!example] Traditional Territorial Clashes (Nagorno-Karabakh)
> The 2020 44-day war between Azerbaijan and Armenia was a classic territorial dispute over an ethnic enclave, modernized by drone technology. The result was massive attrition (Armenia lost 255 tanks and 39 air defense systems to Azerbaijani drones) driven by deeply rooted historical and ethnic grievances.

## 🏚️ 4. State Collapse and Internal Fracturing

Not all wars are fought between states. The 21st century is defined by the catastrophic collapse of fragile governments, leading to multi-faction civil wars fueled by ethnic, religious, and economic disparities.

> [!info] The Vacuum of Power
> When a central government fails to provide basic security, economic stability, or water/food, populations fracture along tribal, ethnic, or sectarian lines. Neighboring states then fund different warlords to secure resources, turning civil wars into regional proxy bloodbaths.

> [!danger] The Humanitarian Cost of Internal Conflict
> - **Sudan (2024–2025):** The collapse of a fragile power-sharing agreement between rival military factions resulted in brutal urban warfare, plunging the country into the world's largest displacement crisis (**14.3 million people displaced**).
> - **Yemen:** The civil war—exacerbated by Saudi and Iranian proxy involvement—resulted in **377,000 deaths by 2021**. Crucially, **59% (223,000)** of these deaths were indirect (famine, disease, collapse of hospitals), illustrating how modern internal conflicts obliterate civilian infrastructure.

## ⚖️ 5. The Security Dilemma and Institutional Failure

The psychological and structural driver of modern conflict is the collapse of trust in the international system to protect sovereign nations.

> [!info] The Security Dilemma
> A concept in international relations where defensive actions by one state (e.g., buying new anti-aircraft missiles or increasing defense budgets) are perceived as offensive threats by a neighboring state. This triggers a reactionary arms race, increasing the likelihood of preemptive war.

> [!danger] The Paralysis of the UN Security Council (UNSC)
> - **The Driver:** The UNSC is structurally paralyzed by the P5 Veto (US, UK, France, Russia, China). Because the P5 routinely veto peacekeeping and sanction resolutions to protect themselves or their allies, the "rules-based international order" has decayed.
> - **The Result:** States no longer trust international law to protect them. This institutional failure drove massive systemic rearmament, such as Germany's **24% defense budget surge to $114 billion in 2025**, and Pakistan's **66% surge in arms imports** (80% supplied by China) leading to the May 2025 border clashes with India.
